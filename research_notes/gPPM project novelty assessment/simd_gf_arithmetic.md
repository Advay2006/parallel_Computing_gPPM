# SIMD Galois-Field (GF(2^w)) Region Arithmetic for Erasure Coding on CPUs: Prior Work

Scope: prior art relevant to a student project that (a) uses SSSE3 PSHUFB nibble tables for GF(2^8), (b) uses AVX2 gathers over per-coefficient byte-contribution tables for GF(2^16)/GF(2^32), (c) has runtime dispatch with scalar fallback, (d) rebuilds four 256-entry tables per GF(2^32) region call (58% of instructions), and (e) times a multiply-vs-XOR cost weight `c` on the host.

Verification legend: "[verified]" means read in a primary source during this session (paper text, source code, or repo listing). "[not verified this session]" means background knowledge or a secondary snippet that I could not confirm against a primary source here.

## Q1. GF-Complete (Plank, Greenan, Miller): techniques for w=8/16/32, and how an AVX2-gather approach compares

### Takeaway
GF-Complete already covers every scalar and SSE technique the project uses for w=8/16/32, and it found shuffle-based SPLIT-TABLE with an alternate memory layout (ALTMAP) to be the fastest SIMD method for every w up to 128. The project's GF(2^32) kernel uses four 256-entry tables built at the start of each call. That is GF-Complete's "lazy SPLIT 8,32" layout almost exactly, with AVX2 gathers doing the lookups instead of scalar loads. I found no published GF-Complete-era evaluation of AVX2 gather for GF arithmetic. General evidence says gather is slow on Intel (no faster than scalar loads), and the 2023 Downfall/GDS microcode mitigation makes it slower still.

### Cited Findings
- Canonical papers: J. S. Plank, K. M. Greenan, E. L. Miller, "Screaming Fast Galois Field Arithmetic Using Intel SIMD Instructions," USENIX FAST 2013. Long version: "A Complete Treatment of Software Implementations of Finite Field Arithmetic for Erasure Coding Applications," Univ. Tennessee Tech Report UT-CS-13-717, 14 Oct 2013. [verified: TR title, authors, date] — [UT-CS-13-717 PDF](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf); [FAST'13 paper (ResearchGate listing)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/260436836_Screaming_Fast_Galois_Field_Arithmetic_Using_Intel_SIMD_Instructions). The usenix.org PDF URLs I tried returned 404. The landing page is presumably https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast13/technical-sessions/presentation/plank_james_screaming [not verified this session].
- Techniques catalogued in the TR: TABLE, LOG-TABLE, SHIFT, CARRY-FREE (PCLMULQDQ), BYTWO_p/BYTWO_b, GROUP, SPLIT-TABLE, COMPOSITE, CAUCHY (bit-matrix/XOR-only). The "tricks" include small-table lookup, Double/Quad tables, Lazy Table Creation, and Alternate Mapping (ALTMAP). [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- Lazy SPLIT-TABLE for GF(2^32) (the project's table design): "a lazy implementation of SPLIT-TABLE in GF(2^32) with ga = 8 and gb = 32 requires four 256-element tables which must be created at the beginning of the multiply_region() operation. Then, every 32-bit word in the product region may be calculated with four table lookups, and either three or four XOR's" (TR §8.3). [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- The TR says this lazy approach depends on amortization: "If the size of the region is large enough, the cost of creating the row of the table is amortized". Of the large-table methods it says "they are created lazily, and thus perform best on large regions that can amortize the table creation." [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- SIMD for w=16 (§8.6.2): SPLIT-TABLE with ga=4 and gb=16 gives four 16-entry tables of 16-bit products. Each is split into high-byte and low-byte tables (8 PSHUFB tables per coefficient). With the standard mapping this takes 8 lookups per 128-bit vector (8 words). With ALTMAP (the high and low bytes of 16 words stored in separate vectors), 8 PSHUFBs multiply 32 bytes, "roughly a factor of two" faster. If the application needs the standard layout, the TR says it is faster to convert to ALTMAP, multiply, and convert back. [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- SIMD for w=32: SPLIT-TABLE with ga=4 and gb=32 plus ALTMAP. "Each 32-bit word is then split across four 16-byte vectors, and 32 lookup tables are required to multiply each 64-byte chunk." The technique extends to w=64 with 128 tables. [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- Results (Fig. 29, peak SIMD multiply_region): "For every value of w, the best performance is achieved by leveraging _mm_shuffle_epi8() with either TABLE (w = 4) or SPLIT-TABLE (the other w). For w > 8, the alternate mapping ... outperforms the standard mapping significantly ... when w ≤ 32, the alternate mappings are cache limited." Performance above roughly 6 GB/s was L3-limited on their machine. CARRY-FREE (PCLMUL) region multiply was shown only for w=64/128 and "In neither case is the implementation competitive". COMPOSITE never beat shuffle-based methods. CAUCHY (XOR-only) was competitive only at w=4. [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- Without SIMD (Fig. 30), table-based methods win for all w except w=4. Tables indexed by 16-bit indices were best except at w=32 and w=128. [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- For single-word multiply, CARRY-FREE with an "alternate" primitive polynomial (CARRY-FREE-ALT) "significantly outperforms" the others for w ≥ 32. [verified, partial text] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- GF-Complete source (ceph mirror, Revision 1.03, 1 Jan 2015) has `gf_w32_split_8_32_lazy_multiply_region`, `split_16_32_lazy`, `split_2_32_lazy(_sse)`, `split_4_32_lazy(_sse)(_altmap)`, plus a `src/neon/` directory (`gf_w4/8/16/32/64_neon.c`). `gf_w32.c` has no AVX/AVX2 or gather code (grep for "avx" and "gather" found nothing). [verified] — [ceph/gf-complete](https://github.com/ceph/gf-complete); [gf_w32.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/gf-complete/master/src/gf_w32.c)
- The original paper's Intel SIMD work is SSE2/SSSE3/SSE4.x/PCLMUL. GF-Complete has no AVX2-gather path. [verified for source tree; the FAST'13 text itself was not readable here]
- No source found benchmarks AVX2 gather against PSHUFB specifically for GF multiplication. General evidence: on Haswell, `_mm256_i32gather_epi32` table lookup was reported "31% slower" than the alternative, and a VPGATHERDD SpMV variant took "1.7x as long" as movd/pinsrw (Stack Overflow archives). A 2021 analysis says vpgatherdd still issues 8 micro-loads internally, so the gather unit is the bottleneck. [secondary sources, verified as search snippets] — [SO: faster lookup tables using avx2](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/35789996/faster-lookup-tables-using-avx2); [SO: avx2 sparse matrix multiplication](https://kiwix.lema.org/stackoverflow.com_en_all_2023-11/questions/31430198/avx2-sparse-matrix-multiplication); [Johnny's SW Lab, AVX2 gather and the memory wall](https://johnnysswlab.com/?p=2014)
- Downfall / Gather Data Sampling (CVE-2022-40982, Aug 2023): Intel's microcode mitigation slows AVX2/AVX-512 gather instructions specifically and is on by default once the microcode is installed. Red Hat reports low single-digit slowdowns for realistic applications but a significant slowdown in a gather-heavy worst-case microbenchmark. Phoronix raised a possible >50% penalty for heavy-gather AVX code (stated as a concern, not a measured result). deal.II developers reported a "dramatic" impact. [verified as advisories/snippets] — [Red Hat GDS advisory](https://access.redhat.com/solutions/7027704); [Phoronix Downfall](https://Phoronix.com/review/downfall); [Phoronix mitigation benchmarks](https://phoronix.com/review/intel-downfall-benchmarks); [deal.II issue #15873](https://github.com/dealii/dealii/issues/15873); [Ubuntu GDS](https://ubuntu.com/security/vulnerabilities/gds-downfall)
- Older work on GF(2^16)/GF(2^32): Greenan, Miller, Schwarz, "Optimizing Galois Field Arithmetic for Diverse Processor Architectures and Applications," MASCOTS 2008. It covers composite fields and log/antilog tables. — [MASCOTS'08 PDF](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~elm/Papers/mascots08.pdf)

### Inferences
- The GF(2^8) SSSE3 nibble-table PSHUFB kernel is the textbook technique. It appears in GF-Complete (SPLIT 4,8), Anvin's RAID-6 work, ISA-L, klauspost/reedsolomon, Leopard and others, so it is not novel.
- The GF(2^32) kernel (four 256-entry 32-bit tables, four lookups and XORs per word) is GF-Complete's lazy SPLIT 8,32 with the scalar lookups swapped for `vpgatherdd`. The GF(2^16) kernel with per-byte-contribution tables is the analogous SPLIT 8,16 / lazy-table design. The only new element is using AVX2 gather for the lookups. Prior evidence suggests this is a regression, not an advance, compared with PSHUFB SPLIT 4,w ALTMAP: one PSHUFB does 32 independent byte lookups per AVX2 instruction, while one gather does 8 lookups through the load ports. It is likely much worse on Intel Skylake through Tiger Lake/Ice Lake parts with the GDS mitigation. The project should benchmark against GF-Complete's `SPLIT 4 16/32 ALTMAP` SSE paths, or an AVX2 port of them, before claiming speed.
- The measured 58% table-rebuild overhead reproduces the amortization problem GF-Complete documents. It means the region sizes per call are too small for the lazy-table strategy. GF-Complete mitigates this with a single-entry cache (see Q4).
- GF(2^32) via PCLMUL (CARRY-FREE) is not competitive for region multiplication per GF-Complete (2013 hardware). VPCLMULQDQ on AVX-512/AVX2 (Ice Lake and later, Zen 3/4) may change this. I found no erasure-coding evaluation of VPCLMULQDQ for w=32 region multiply.

### Gaps
- I could not open the FAST'13 PDF itself (404 at the usenix.org URLs tried), so its exact throughput numbers per w and its machine (the TR figures were read only as bar labels) are not transcribed here. The TR text confirms which method is fastest but its bar-chart values were not extractable.
- No primary-source benchmark of AVX2/AVX-512 gather for GF(2^16)/GF(2^32) region multiply was found. The claim that gather is slower comes from general gather benchmarks and is an inference for GF specifically.

## Q2. Intel ISA-L erasure_code: instructions, fields, throughput

### Takeaway
ISA-L supports only GF(2^8). It provides multi-output dot-product and "mad" (multiply-and-add) kernels for SSE, AVX, AVX2, AVX-512, AVX2+GFNI and AVX-512+GFNI on x86, NEON and SVE on aarch64, and RVV on RISC-V, all behind a runtime multibinary dispatcher. Per-coefficient 32-byte tables are precomputed once by `ec_init_tables` and reused across calls.

### Cited Findings
- README: "Fast block Reed-Solomon type erasure codes for any encode/decode matrix in GF(2^8)". Build requirements: nasm ≥ 2.14.01 on x86_64, gas on aarch64, gas ≥ 2.39 for RISC-V Vector (RVV). [verified] — [intel/isa-l](https://github.com/intel/isa-l)
- The erasure_code directory has `gf_{,2..6}vect_dot_prod_avx512(_gfni).asm`, `gf_{,2..6}vect_mad_avx512(_gfni).asm`, `gf_{,2..6}vect_dot_prod_avx2_gfni.asm`, `gf_{,2..6}vect_mad_avx2_gfni.asm`, `gf_vect_mul_avx2_gfni.asm` and `gf_vect_gfni.inc`. The aarch64 directory has `gf_*vect_dot_prod_neon.S`, `gf_*vect_mad_neon.S`, `gf_*vect_mad_sve.S`, `gf_nvect_dot_prod_sve.c`, `gf_vect_mul_{neon,sve}.S` and `ec_aarch64_dispatcher.c`. [verified via GitHub API listing] — [isa-l/erasure_code](https://github.com/intel/isa-l/tree/master/erasure_code)
- GFNI (AVX-512 and AVX2) erasure-code kernels and a new SVE implementation arrived in ISA-L v2.31 (released Jan 2024). The commits landed Nov–Dec 2023 (Marcel Cornu, Pablo de Lara). [verified via wiki/release snippets] — [ISA-L v2.31.0 release](https://github.com/intel/isa-l/releases/tag/v2.31.0); [Debian copy of Release_notes.txt](https://sources.debian.org/src/libisal/2.31.1-3/Release_notes.txt)
- API: `ec_init_tables(k, rows, a, gftbls)` expands the coding coefficients into `gftbls`, which "Must be of size 32*k*rows". So each coefficient gets 32 bytes (two 16-byte nibble tables). `ec_encode_data(len, k, rows, gftbls, data, coding)` then uses those precomputed tables. [verified] — [erasure_code.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/intel/isa-l/master/include/erasure_code.h)
- Throughput: the ISA-L repo gives no figures. An SC21 paper cites ISA-L at ~6.7 GB/s in its comparison and reports its own XOR-based library at 8.92 GB/s, beating ISA-L on the paper's testbed. [verified as abstract] — [Uezato, SC21, arXiv 2108.02692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02692)

### Inferences
- ISA-L's design (precompute per-coefficient tables once, fuse several outputs per pass with `Nvect_dot_prod`/`mad`, dispatch at runtime from SSE to AVX-512/GFNI) is the industrial state of the art for GF(2^8). The project's runtime dispatch with scalar fallback is standard practice, not novel.
- ISA-L deliberately avoids GF(2^16) and GF(2^32), which makes ISA-L comparisons valid only for the project's GF(2^8) path.

### Gaps
- No official ISA-L GB/s numbers for GFNI vs AVX2 were found. Intel performance whitepapers for EC were not located this session.

## Q3. Other SIMD GF libraries and papers

### Takeaway
SIMD GF(2^8) shuffles are ubiquitous (Anvin 2004 onward). GF(2^16) has well-developed SIMD implementations: GF-Complete ALTMAP, Leopard-RS (FFT-based, GF(2^8)/GF(2^16)), klauspost/reedsolomon Leopard-GF16 mode, and ParPar/par2cmdline-turbo (PAR2 GF(2^16), with PSHUFB, XOR-JIT, CLMUL and GFNI methods). GFNI (`GF2P8AFFINEQB`) is now the fastest x86 path in ISA-L, klauspost and Leopard. None of these use AVX2 gather as the primary GF(2^16) technique, as far as I could find.

### Cited Findings
- **H. Peter Anvin, "The mathematics of RAID-6"** (Linux kernel; first 2004, later revisions). Introduced fast multiply-by-2 with SIMD and the PSHUFB nibble-table approach for RAID-6 recovery (`raid6_recov_ssse3`). [not verified this session] — https://www.kernel.org/pub/linux/kernel/people/hpa/raid6.pdf
- The GF-Complete TR cites "Anvin's optimization" for multiply-by-two in SIMD (§8.5, Fig. 28). [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- **Linux kernel lib/raid6 (pq.h/algos.c)**: at boot it benchmarks each available RAID-6 `gen()` implementation, prints `raid6: %-8s gen() %5ld MB/s`, then reports "using algorithm %s gen() %ld MB/s" and selects a recovery algorithm (`raid6_choose_gen`, `raid6_choose_recov`). [verified, v6.6 source] — [lib/raid6/algos.c @ v6.6](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.6/lib/raid6/algos.c)
- **Jerasure 2.0** (Plank, Greenan, Miller et al., 2014) is built on GF-Complete. It inherits the SIMD region kernels and adds matrix/bitmatrix codes and schedules. — [GF-Complete TR front matter](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf); [ceph/gf-complete](https://github.com/ceph/gf-complete). (The Jerasure 2.0 TR UT-CS-14-721 was not fetched this session.)
- **klauspost/reedsolomon (Go)**: "SSSE3, AVX2 and AVX512 assembly are available on AMD64"; "will use GFNI instructions combined with AVX512 when these are available", improving speed "by up to 2x over AVX2 code paths"; NEON on arm64 and accelerated ppc64le. Fields: GF(2^8) default, Leopard-compatible GF(2^8), and Leopard GF16 for >256 shards (up to 65,536 shards). Example AMD64 numbers (MB/s, 1 MB shards, 32 threads, machine unnamed), 10+4: Go 17,999; SSSE3 80,093; AVX2 152,673; GFNI 273,628. Graviton2 single-core NEON Galois1M: 12,569 MB/s. It uses an inversion cache. A `nopshufb` build tag removes PSHUFB code. [verified] — [klauspost/reedsolomon](https://github.com/klauspost/reedsolomon)
- **Leopard-RS (catid)**: FFT-based Reed-Solomon over GF(2^8) and GF(2^16) using the Lin–Al-Naffouri–Han–Chung (2016) polynomial-basis FFT. The current README (2026 revision) says "SSSE3, AVX2, AVX-512VL, and GFNI kernels are separate translation units selected only after CPU and OS checks". AUTO uses GF8 up to 256 shards and GF16 above. [verified; README appears substantially revised by 2026] — [catid/leopard](https://github.com/catid/leopard)
- Original Leopard design: GF(2^16) multiply via PSHUFB split nibble tables (4 nibbles × lo/hi byte tables) with precomputed per-log-value tables, O(n log n) encode. Sibling catid projects: CM256 (Cauchy MDS GF(2^8), SSSE3/AVX2/NEON), longhair (Cauchy RS bit-matrix, GF(2^8)), gf256 (SIMD GF(2^8) library), fecal, wirehair, siamese. [not verified this session] — https://github.com/catid/cm256, https://github.com/catid/longhair, https://github.com/catid/gf256
- **ParPar (animetosho), "fast-gf-multiplication.md"**: surveys GF(2^16) region-multiply techniques. Vector split lookup (PSHUFB/VTBL; 8 lookup vectors for w=16, 4 with interleaved/ALTMAP layout, free interleave on NEON via `VLD2`). XOR-JIT ("often out-performing the more well-known Vector Split Lookup Table method (at least on x86 for w=16)", but with "High overhead for each invocation ... performs very poorly across small regions"). CLMUL with deferred reduction ("Carry-less multiplication is slow on a number of processors"). GFNI affine (w=16 split into four 8×8 matrices, "half the number of operations and half the lookup tables" of vector split). The page does not mention gather. [verified] — [ParPar fast-gf-multiplication.md](https://github.com/animetosho/ParPar/blob/master/fast-gf-multiplication.md)
- **Uezato, "Accelerating XOR-based Erasure Coding using Program Optimization Techniques," SC'21** (DOI 10.1145/3458817.3476204). Uses straight-line programs, grammar compression, deforestation and red-blue pebble games to reach 8.92 GB/s, beating ISA-L. — [arXiv 2108.02692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02692); [SC21 page](https://sc21.supercomputing.org/proceedings/tech_paper/tech_paper_pages/pap462.html)
- **Zhou & Tian, "Fast Erasure Coding for Data Storage: A Comprehensive Study of the Acceleration Techniques," USENIX FAST'19** (and ACM TOS 2020; open-source "Zerasure"). Bitmatrix optimization under a weighted cost function, XOR-reduction scheduling, cache management and vectorized XOR. — [FAST'19 page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast19/presentation/zhou); [Zhou dissertation 2020](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/bitstream/1969.1/191951/1/ZHOU-DISSERTATION-2020.pdf)
- **Erasure-coding survey (P. P. C. Lee et al., ACM TOS 2024)** covers EC acceleration techniques broadly. [not read this session] — [tos24ecsurvey.pdf](https://www.cse.cuhk.edu.hk/~pclee/www/pubs/tos24ecsurvey.pdf)
- **Backblaze JavaReedSolomon** (2015): a pure-Java GF(2^8) table-based implementation, notable for loop-ordering benchmarks, not SIMD. [not verified this session] — https://github.com/Backblaze/JavaReedSolomon
- **GFNI**: `GF2P8AFFINEQB` / `GF2P8MULB` (Ice Lake/Tremont and later, Zen 4) give an 8×8 bit-matrix multiply per byte. One affine instruction multiplies 64 bytes by a GF(2^8) constant (AVX-512), replacing 2 PSHUFB plus nibble shifts and masks. Used by ISA-L ≥ 2.31, klauspost and Leopard. [verified usage in these repos; instruction semantics not verified this session]
- **ARM**: GF-Complete NEON (`src/neon/*`), ISA-L NEON and SVE (≥ 2.31), and klauspost NEON (verified above). Huawei Kunpeng ships an ISA-L port. [verified for repos] — [Kunpeng ISA-L doc](https://www.hikunpeng.com/document/detail/en/kunpengaccel/storage/isal/README_EN.md)

### Inferences
- SIMD dispatch with scalar fallback, GF(2^8) PSHUFB and GF(2^16) SIMD are all mature, widely deployed prior art. The project's kernels are re-implementations at best.
- The most competitive design for GF(2^16) on AVX2 hardware without GFNI is the ALTMAP/interleaved split-nibble PSHUFB (GF-Complete, Leopard, ParPar), or XOR-JIT for large regions. With GFNI, the affine method roughly halves the work. Gather-based lookup is outside the mainstream and probably slower.
- For GF(2^32), no mainstream erasure-coding library besides GF-Complete targets it with SIMD. GF-Complete's SSE SPLIT 4,32 ALTMAP (32 PSHUFB tables per 64-byte chunk) is the direct comparator for the project's AVX2 gather kernel.

### Gaps
- I did not verify current CM256/longhair/gf256/Backblaze repo details or Anvin's paper URL this session.
- I found no peer-reviewed paper specifically about GFNI for erasure coding. GFNI support is documented only in library code and release notes.

## Q4. Prior work on per-coefficient table caching/reuse for GF(2^16)/GF(2^32) split tables

### Takeaway
Table reuse is established prior art. GF-Complete keeps a single-entry cache (`last_value`) and rebuilds the lazy split tables only when the multiplier changes. ISA-L precomputes all per-coefficient tables once (`ec_init_tables`, 32 B per coefficient) and reuses them for every encode call. Leopard and ParPar precompute per-multiplier lookup vectors. Adding caching to the project's GF(2^32) kernel would be an expected engineering fix, not a contribution.

### Cited Findings
- GF-Complete `gf_w32_split_8_32_lazy_multiply_region`: `change = (val != d8->last_value); if (change) d8->last_value = val;` The four 256-entry tables (`t[i][k^j] = v ^ t[i][k]`, built by doubling) are rebuilt only `if (change)`. The same `last_value` check appears in `split_16_32_lazy`, `split_2_32_lazy` and `split_4_32_lazy`. [verified] — [gf_w32.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceph/gf-complete/master/src/gf_w32.c)
- GF-Complete TR §8.3 frames the issue as amortizing per-call table creation over large regions. [verified] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf)
- ISA-L: `ec_init_tables(k, rows, a, gftbls)` produces precomputed tables of size `32*k*rows` that are passed to `ec_encode_data`, `ec_encode_data_update` and others. [verified] — [erasure_code.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/intel/isa-l/master/include/erasure_code.h)
- ParPar notes that for split lookup, "Requires computing a (w/8)*256 entry lookup table", and that for w=8 "since there are only 256 possible tables, all can be generated beforehand". XOR-JIT has high per-invocation overhead for small regions. [verified] — [ParPar fast-gf-multiplication.md](https://github.com/animetosho/ParPar/blob/master/fast-gf-multiplication.md)
- klauspost/reedsolomon caches inverted (reconstruction) matrices, but says "caching reconstruction matrices generally isn't feasible for Leopard" at high shard counts. That is a matrix-level cache, not a table cache. [verified] — [klauspost/reedsolomon](https://github.com/klauspost/reedsolomon)

### Inferences
- In gPPM/SD-code encoding, the coefficient matrix is fixed per code, so all per-coefficient tables could be precomputed once, ISA-L style. At 4×256×4 B = 4 KB per coefficient for GF(2^32) split-8, this is cache-hungry for large matrices. That trade-off is why GF-Complete prefers SPLIT 4,32 (32 × 16-byte tables = 512 B per coefficient) for SIMD.
- A one-entry or multi-entry cache keyed by coefficient would remove most of the 58% overhead when the same coefficient repeats across sectors/stripes. GF-Complete already does exactly the one-entry version.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that studies the table-cache policy itself (e.g., LRU over coefficients) for erasure coding. The prior art is in code, not in a dedicated evaluation.

## Q5. Prior work on automatically calibrating multiply-vs-XOR cost on hardware to choose schedules or sequences

### Takeaway
Runtime timing to choose among implementations is old practice. Linux RAID-6 and the XOR template code have benchmarked candidates at boot for decades, though they choose a kernel, not a schedule. Erasure-coding schedule research uses analytic weighted cost functions (Zhou & Tian's weighted XOR/memory cost) or XOR counts, and later work stresses that operation counts poorly predict throughput. I found no prior paper that measures a per-field multiply-XOR/XOR cost ratio on the host and feeds it into a matrix-sequence selection like gPPM's. This is the most plausibly novel element of the project, but it is incremental, and gPPM itself already selects calculation sequences dynamically.

### Cited Findings
- Linux `lib/raid6/algos.c`: `raid6_choose_gen()` times each candidate `gen()` over `1<<RAID6_TIME_JIFFIES_LG2` jiffies, prints MB/s, and picks the best. `raid6_choose_recov()` picks a recovery algorithm. `crypto/xor.c` has `do_xor_speed()` and `calibrate_xor_blocks()`, which print each template's MB/sec and select the fastest. [verified, v6.6] — [lib/raid6/algos.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.6/lib/raid6/algos.c); [crypto/xor.c](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.6/crypto/xor.c)
- klauspost/reedsolomon has `WithAutoGoroutines(shardSize)`, which "will attempt to calculate the optimal number of goroutines". It does not select code paths by benchmarking. [verified] — [klauspost/reedsolomon](https://github.com/klauspost/reedsolomon)
- Zhou & Tian (FAST'19) optimize the bitmatrix "by minimizing a weighted cost function" and then schedule with XOR reduction and cache management. I could not confirm whether the weights are measured on hardware. [verified as abstract only] — [FAST'19](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast19/presentation/zhou); [dissertation](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/bitstream/1969.1/191951/1/ZHOU-DISSERTATION-2020.pdf)

(DSN 2012 heuristics; Luo/Xu/Plank XOR scheduling) note that minimizing the XOR count of a schedule is open, and that cache ordering has a "significant, but hard-to-quantify" effect on performance. [verified: the GF-Complete TR §8.6.1 quote; DSN'12 page from search] — [UT-CS-13-717](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/UT-CS-13-717.pdf); [DSN 2012 heuristics](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/DSN-2012.html)

Reported results: up to 102.41% over PPM and 32.25% over RS. PPM improved SD/LRC speed by up to 210.81%. ### Inferences
- Timing primitives at startup to parameterize a decision is standard (Linux raid6/xor, FFTW-style autotuning). Applying it to estimate gPPM's cost weight `c` per field is a reasonable, modest engineering extension. Its novelty depends on whether gPPM's paper already treats the multiply/XOR cost as a fixed constant. That needs checking in the gPPM full text, which I did not obtain.
- Because GF(2^32) multiply-XOR cost depends heavily on implementation (lazy table rebuild, gather vs PSHUFB, GDS mitigation), a calibrated `c` will mostly measure the project's own kernel inefficiencies. A better kernel would shrink `c` and change which sequences gPPM chooses. Report `c` alongside the kernel used.

### Gaps
- I could not read the gPPM paper text to see how it models multiply vs XOR cost (constant or parameter), or whether it calibrates.
- No paper was found that auto-calibrates multiply/XOR cost ratios to choose erasure-coding schedules. Absence of evidence after limited search (about 15 queries), not proof of absence.
