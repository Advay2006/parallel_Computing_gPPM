# Prior work on calculation-sequence/schedule optimization and multicore parallelization of matrix-based erasure coding (for gPPM project novelty assessment)

Scope: (1) reordering/scheduling of erasure-code matrix computations to reduce operations, including "matrix-first" (precomputed decoding matrix) vs "data-first" ordering; (2) cost models and hardware calibration; (3) multicore/multithreaded parallelization. Research date: 2026-11-15. Items marked [UNVERIFIED] were not confirmed against a primary source during this session.

## Q1. XOR scheduling for bit-matrix codes: what the classic works do, and how matrix-first vs data-first ordering relates

### Takeaway
The XOR-scheduling literature (Huang et al. 2007; Plank et al. DSN 2012; Luo et al. TC 2014; Zhou & Tian FAST 2019) optimizes the order and sharing of XORs *within a single fixed bit-matrix* (common-subexpression elimination, cache-aware ordering, bitmatrix selection under a cost function). None of these works, as far as I could verify, frames the problem as choosing between "apply S then F^-1 to data" and "compute G = F^-1*S first, then one data pass". That choice is gPPM's own contribution (and before it, PPM, ICPP 2015), not the student project's.

### Cited Findings
- **gPPM itself**: Shiyi Li, Qiang Cao, Shenggang Wan, Wen Xia, Changsheng Xie, "gPPM: A Generalized Matrix Operation and Parallel Algorithm to Accelerate the Encoding/Decoding Process of Erasure Codes," ACM TACO 20(4), Dec 2023, DOI 10.1145/3625005. Abstract: gPPM surpasses PPM "by employing fine-grained dynamic matrix calculation sequence selection," and unlike PPM it also applies to RS codes; reports up to 102.41% over PPM and up to 32.25% over RS — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3625005); [HIT Scholar record](https://scholar.hit.edu.cn/en/publications/gppm-a-generalized-matrix-operation-and-parallel-algorithm-to-acc/); [researchr bibtex](https://researchr.org/publication/LiCWXX23/bibtex). (Full text at dl.acm.org returned HTTP 403 to my fetcher; I could not read the body.)
- **Predecessor PPM**: S. Li, Q. Cao, S. Wan, W. Zhang, C. Xie, X. He, P. Subedi, "PPM: A Partitioned and Parallel Matrix Algorithm to Accelerate Encoding/Decoding Process of Asymmetric Parity Erasure Codes," ICPP 2015, pp. 460–469, DOI 10.1109/ICPP.2015.55. Abstract: the parity-check-matrix method "is serial and generates high computational cost"; PPM partitions the parity-check matrix, parallelizes the encoding/decoding operations, and optimizes the calculation sequence; up to 210.81% speedup for SD, PMDS, LRC codes — [dblp](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/conf/icpp/LiCWZXHS15.html); [ISC record](https://search.isc.ac/inventory/49/3706520.htm). So "partition into independent sub-matrices + parallel execution + sequence optimization" dates to 2015.
- **Huang, Li, Chen (ITW 2007)**: Cheng Huang, Jin Li, Minghua Chen, "On Optimizing XOR-Based Codes for Fault-Tolerant Storage Applications," IEEE Information Theory Workshop 2007. Introduces "common operations first" (compute shared XOR sub-expressions once and reuse); formulates a generic optimization problem conjectured NP-complete; proposes two greedy (matching-based) algorithms; optimized XOR-based RS beat EVENODD for 2-fault tolerance — [Microsoft Research page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=322550); [US patent 8,209,577](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8209577). Zhou & Tian describe it as building a graph where edge weight between vertices i, j counts shared XOR pairs, then using maximum-cardinality matching — [Zhou & Tian FAST'19 PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf).
- **Plank, Schuman, Robison**: "Heuristics for Optimizing Matrix-Based Erasure Codes for Fault-Tolerant Storage Systems," **DSN 2012** (not FAST 2012 as stated in the assignment), Boston, June 2012; earlier tech report UT-CS-10-664 (Dec 2010). States that deriving an XOR-minimal schedule from a bit-matrix is an open problem; proposes two heuristics, **Uber-CHRS** (spelled "CHRS" on Plank's page, not "CSHR") and **X-Sets**, outperforming earlier heuristics; open-source code released — [Plank DSN-2012 page](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/DSN-2012.html); [TR CS-10-664](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/CS-10-664.html).
- **Plank et al. bitmatrix scheduling in Jerasure** ("smart" scheduling: derive a row from a previously computed row when cheaper) is implemented as `jerasure_smart_bitmatrix_to_schedule`, used by `jerasure_generate_decoding_schedule(..., smart)` — [Jerasure jerasure.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tsuraan/Jerasure/master/src/jerasure.c).
- **Plank, "XOR's, Lower Bounds and MDS Codes for Storage"**: Zhou & Tian cite Plank's work as having found "lower bounds on the total number of XOR's" — [Zhou & Tian FAST'19](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf). [UNVERIFIED: exact venue; my recollection is IEEE ITW 2011, Paraty, Brazil — not confirmed this session.]
- **Luo, Shrestha, Xu, Plank**: "Efficient Encoding Schedules for XOR-Based Erasure Codes," IEEE Transactions on Computers 63(9):2259–2272, Sept 2014, DOI 10.1109/TC.2013.23. Finds CPU cache efficiency strongly affects encoding speed; proposes XOR scheduling algorithms that improve cache use; beats conventional ordering on several platforms — [ISC record](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/49/83774.htm). Conference precursor: Luo, Xu, Plank, "An efficient XOR-scheduling algorithm for erasure codes encoding," DSN 2009 — same source. Zhou & Tian summarize: "the order of operations in the computation schedule ... can affect the performance, due to CPU cache miss penalty" — [Zhou & Tian](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf).
- **Zhou & Tian (FAST 2019)**: Tianli Zhou, Chao Tian, "Fast Erasure Coding for Data Storage: A Comprehensive Study of the Acceleration Techniques," USENIX FAST '19, pp. 317–329. Jointly applies bitmatrix optimization (minimizing a weighted cost function, via genetic algorithm), XOR reduction (Huang-style matching, unweighted/weighted), scheduling/caching management (Luo-style), and XOR-level vectorization; concludes vectorizing XORs on the bitmatrix beats vectorizing GF operations directly; competitive with EVENODD, RDP, STAR — [USENIX page](https://usenix.org/conference/fast19/presentation/zhou); [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf). Third-party summary: XOR-level vectorization gave the largest gain (avg ~130%), other techniques 4.8–36.6% individually — [Keyun Cheng notes](https://keyuncheng.github.io/posts/2020/08/paper-reading-ec-acceleration-fast19) (secondary). Zhou & Tian treat decoding as "of secondary importance" because full decode only occurs on degraded reads — [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf). Extended in Zhou's 2020 TAMU dissertation — [dissertation](https://oaktrust.library.tamu.edu/bitstream/1969.1/191951/1/ZHOU-DISSERTATION-2020.pdf). [UNVERIFIED: the ACM TOS 2020 journal version's exact citation was not retrieved.]
- **Hafner, Deenadhayalan, Rao, Tomlin (FAST 2005)**: "Matrix Methods for Lost Data Reconstruction in Erasure Codes," FAST '05, pp. 183–196. Uses matrix theory and pseudo-inverses to produce, for each lost sector, either "unrecoverable" or an explicit reconstruction formula (i.e., the reconstruction matrix is derived on coefficients before touching data); also a hybrid for partial-strip recovery — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast-05/matrix-methods-lost-data-reconstruction-erasure-codes); [PDF](https://static.usenix.org/event/fast05/tech/full_papers/hafner_matrix/hafner_matrix.pdf).

### Inferences
- The XOR-scheduling line operates at bit-level within one matrix–data product; gPPM's normal-vs-matrix-first choice is at the level of *association order of a matrix chain* (F^-1 · (S · B_S) vs (F^-1 · S) · B_S). This is the classic matrix-chain association problem applied to GF matrices where one operand is huge (data); the cost asymmetry (small coefficient matrices vs large data regions) makes matrix-first usually win unless G is much denser than S and F^-1 combined. This framing is not found in the scheduling papers above, but it is a well-known concept in linear algebra; its application per-submatrix with a sparsity-based cost is attributable to PPM/gPPM, not to the student project.
- Huang's "common operations first" is conceptually adjacent to data-first ordering: computing S·B_S first and reusing intermediate results is itself a form of sharing intermediate products, which can win when G = F^-1 S is dense.

## Q2. Is precomputing the decoding matrix F^-1*S ("matrix-first") standard practice in RS libraries?

### Takeaway
Yes. Both major libraries precompute a combined decoding matrix on small coefficient matrices before a single pass over data: ISA-L's documented decode recipe multiplies the inverted survivor matrix by the encode rows for lost parity, and Jerasure's bit-matrix scheduled decoder builds "one decoding matrix which is going to decode everything with one call." Jerasure's GF-word decoder is a fixed hybrid (inverse rows for lost data, then re-encode lost parity from recovered data). What is *not* standard in these libraries is a per-sub-matrix, cost-driven choice between the two orders — that is gPPM's contribution.

### Cited Findings
- **ISA-L**: the official example builds the decode matrix as follows: invert the k×k survivor submatrix (`gf_invert_matrix`), copy rows of the inverse for lost data fragments, and "For non-src (parity) erasures need to multiply encode matrix * invert" (computing `s ^= gf_mul(invert_matrix[j*k+i], encode_matrix[k*frag_err_list[p]+j])` on coefficients only); then `ec_init_tables(k, nerrs, decode_matrix, g_tbls)` and a single `ec_encode_data(len, k, nerrs, g_tbls, recover_srcs, recover_outp)` pass over the data — [ISA-L examples/ec/ec_simple_example.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/intel/isa-l/master/examples/ec/ec_simple_example.c). This is exactly G = F^-1·S computed on the coefficient matrices first.
- **Jerasure (GF-word path)**: `jerasure_make_decoding_matrix(k, m, w, matrix, erased, decoding_matrix, dm_ids)` inverts the survivor matrix; `jerasure_matrix_decode` then applies rows of that inverse via `jerasure_matrix_dotprod` to recover lost data, and recomputes lost coding devices by re-encoding from (now complete) data with the original matrix rows — [Jerasure jerasure.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tsuraan/Jerasure/master/src/jerasure.c). This is data-first for lost parity (two data passes in sequence), matrix-first for lost data.
- **Jerasure (bit-matrix scheduled path)**: `jerasure_generate_decoding_schedule` comments: "we're going to create one decoding matrix which is going to decode everything with one call. The hope is that the scheduler will do a good job." For lost coding devices it composes the distribution-matrix rows with the inverse rows on the bit-matrix (zeroing failed-data columns and XORing in decoding rows) — i.e., matrix-first, with the composed matrix then scheduled (optionally "smart") — [Jerasure jerasure.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tsuraan/Jerasure/master/src/jerasure.c). Jerasure also offers `jerasure_generate_schedule_cache` to precompute decoding schedules for all 1- and 2-erasure patterns — same source.
- **Hafner et al. (FAST 2005)** similarly derive reconstruction formulas from (pseudo-)inverse matrices before data reconstruction — [USENIX](https://www.usenix.org/conference/fast-05/matrix-methods-lost-data-reconstruction-erasure-codes).

### Inferences
- "Matrix-first" by itself is standard practice and should not be claimed as novel by the project (nor, strictly, by gPPM). Jerasure's GF path shows the "normal"/sequential order also appears in practice, so both orders exist as fixed library policies; gPPM's novelty is the adaptive, fine-grained selection by a sparsity cost model.
- For the student project, implementing the sequence selector is reproduction of gPPM; any novelty must come from the calibration and the parallelization strategy.

## Q3. Cost models weighting multiply vs XOR; hardware-calibrated cost models / autotuning

### Takeaway
There is direct precedent for empirically calibrating operation weights in an erasure-coding cost model on the target machine: Zhou & Tian (FAST 2019) set the XOR-vs-copy weight by timing memcpy vs XOR on their workstation (≈1.5×) and found the calibrated weighted cost best predicted throughput (0–10% gains). gPPM's C = u + c·v follows the same pattern (count-based proxy with a weight for expensive GF multiplications). Calibrating c by timing on the host is therefore an incremental, well-precedented engineering refinement rather than a new idea; I found no peer-reviewed autotuner for erasure-coding thread count/block size.

### Cited Findings
- Zhou & Tian consider three cost functions: (1) total XORs, (2) total XORs + copies, (3) "a weighted combination of the number of XORs and that of copying operations. In the last option, we set the weight according to the empirical testing result of these operation on the target workstation: the time taken for copying (memcpy) and that for XORing the same amount of data are measured. On our platform, the weight given to XOR is roughly 1.5 the weight given to memory copying." The weighted (calibrated) cost "most accurately capture[s] the encoding computation cost in practice," with improvements "ranging from 0%−10%" and occasional minor degradation — [Zhou & Tian FAST'19 PDF, §4.3](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/fast19-zhou.pdf).
- Plank et al. and Huang et al. use XOR count as the cost metric for schedules — [Plank DSN-2012](https://web.eecs.utk.edu/~jplank/plank/papers/DSN-2012.html); [Huang ITW'07](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=322550).
- Adaptive/benchmark-driven tuning exists in patents: "Storage systems with adaptive erasure code generation" describes obtaining hardware configuration at startup, launching a variable number of coding threads, a benchmark engine populating a performance database, and a monitor adjusting parameters from observed performance — [USPTO 9,292,384](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9292384) (patent, not peer-reviewed).
- Empirical parameter tuning in production: Cloudera's HDFS-EC benchmarks found single-threaded 64/128 KB buffers ~8% faster than 1024 KB (cache effects), but with ≥5 concurrent threads 1024 KB was best, which drove the 1024 KB default cell size — [Cloudera blog](https://www.cloudera.com/blog/technical/hdfs-erasure-coding-in-production.html).
- Founsure gives rule-of-thumb thread counts (e.g., threads = number of failure domains for encoding) rather than a model — [Founsure arXiv 1702.07409](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.07409).

### Inferences
- The project's "calibrate c by timing" mirrors Zhou & Tian's calibration of the XOR/copy weight almost exactly in spirit; it should be described as applying a known technique to gPPM's cost model. It can still be a legitimate contribution if the project shows that gPPM's fixed c mis-selects sequences on modern SIMD hardware (e.g., ISA-L/GF-Complete-style split-table multiplies make "v" much cheaper than older table-lookup multiplies) and quantifies the selection-accuracy gain.

### Gaps
- Did not find what value/method gPPM uses for c (full text not accessible).
- Found no peer-reviewed autotuning framework for CPU erasure coding that jointly selects code, block size and thread count; absence of evidence, not proof of absence.
- Plank, Greenan, Miller, "Screaming Fast Galois Field Arithmetic Using Intel SIMD Instructions," FAST 2013 (GF-Complete) is relevant to the relative cost of multiply vs XOR on SIMD hardware — [UNVERIFIED this session; citation from memory].

## Q4. Multicore parallel erasure coding: data-parallel splitting, pipelining, and evidence that more threads hurt

### Takeaway
Multithreading erasure coding by splitting data (stripes, blocks, or byte ranges) or by output (one thread per parity block) is long-established and considered "embarrassingly parallel"; reported CPU speedups are modest (≈3× for Jerasure+OpenMP, <4 GB/s peak on Xeon) and production systems note diminishing benefit at high concurrency, consistent with a memory-bandwidth-bound workload. Parallelizing independent sub-matrices was already in PPM (2015). Splitting the dependent remainder's data regions into byte ranges across threads is standard data-parallelism; I found no evidence it is new, though its specific combination with gPPM's sequence selection may not have been evaluated before.

### Cited Findings
- **Jerasure 2.0 + OpenMP/pthreads**: "OpenMP and POSIX threads Implementation of Jerasure 2.0 Erasure Coding Library" — encoding/decoding engines "comprise independent 'for' loop iterations and hence possess huge potential for multi-threaded processing"; each parity block computed by a separate thread over a shared data buffer; "upto 3× speedup relative to single-threaded runs"; with larger m "better speedup performance but worse efficiency" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321018556_OpenMP_and_POSIX_threads_Implementation_of_Jerasure_20_Erasure_Coding_Library) (authors/venue not confirmed this session [UNVERIFIED: likely Arslan et al., ~2017]).
- **2025 hardware/software offload paper** (Future Generation Computer Systems, ScienceDirect S0167739X25004819): states OpenMP/POSIX multithreading of Jerasure reaches peak "less than 4 GB/s for standalone EC on Xeon processors" — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167739X25004819) (via search snippet; full text not read).
- **Founsure 1.0**: parallelizes with OpenMP; notes that during pure encoding "data traverses only between CPU caches and the main memory"; ties thread count to output files/hardware threads — [arXiv 1702.07409](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.07409).
- **HDFS-EC with ISA-L**: ISA-L coder ~16× faster than the pure-Java coder single-threaded but only ~8× faster at 40 threads (and 70× vs 35× against the legacy coder) — the advantage shrinks with concurrency; buffer-size optimum shifts with thread count — [Cloudera blog](https://www.cloudera.com/blog/technical/hdfs-erasure-coding-in-production.html).
- **OpenCL erasure coding on heterogeneous architectures (ASAP 2016)**: reports single-thread ISA-L throughput and multithreaded ISA-L comparisons; describes RS coding as compute-bound but notes table lookups consume memory bandwidth, and adds multithreading on top of ISA-L with coarse-grained partitioning to reduce bandwidth pressure — [PDF](https://hzhou.wordpress.ncsu.edu/files/2022/12/asap16.pdf) (via search snippet; details not fully verified).
- **AMD EPYC 7601 white paper**: RS(10,4) single-core encode >5 GB/s at 4 KB and 4096 KB blocks; multicore tests pin each thread to a core for memory locality and relate results to memory channels — [Gigabyte white paper](https://www.gigabyte.com/FileUpload/Global/multimedia/101/file/519/940.pdf) (full scaling numbers not retrieved).
- **ISA-L at MSST 2017**: ~5 GB/s EC on E5-2600 v4 — [Tucker, MSST 2017 slides](https://msstconference.org/MSST-history/2017/Presentations/Tucker-1.pdf) (core count not stated).
- **Large-stripe thesis (Haddock)**: ten ISA-L threads needed ~9 GiB more memory than a single Gibraltar (GPU) thread — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.5555/3338075.3338088).
- **Multicore/accelerator mapping**: Wyrzykowski, Kuczyński, Woźniak, "Towards Efficient Execution of Erasure Codes on Multicore Architectures," PARA 2010, LNCS 7134 (2012), pp. 357–367 — maps RS and Cauchy RS onto Cell/B.E. and GPUs — [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/towards-efficient-execution-of-erasure-codes-on-multicore-archit/3832652).
- **GPU comparison**: PErasure (ICC 2015) reports raw GPU coding ~10× faster than multithreaded Jerasure on a quad-core CPU; describes CRS as memory-intensive, bounded by GPU global-memory bandwidth — [PErasure PDF](https://www.comp.hkbu.edu.hk/~chxw/papers/icc_2015.pdf).
- **PPM (ICPP 2015)** explicitly "parallelizes the encoding/decoding operations" over the partitioned matrix — [dblp](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/rec/conf/icpp/LiCWZXHS15.html); [ISC](https://search.isc.ac/inventory/49/3706520.htm).

### Inferences
- Threads over independent sub-matrices ≈ PPM/gPPM's own "parallel" component; byte-range splitting of the dependent remainder ≈ the standard data-parallel strategy used by OpenMP Jerasure/Founsure/ISA-L wrappers. Neither is novel in isolation.
- The low arithmetic intensity of GF/XOR coding (each data byte touched a few times per output) implies throughput plateaus once DRAM bandwidth saturates; the HDFS concurrency results and modest (~3×) OpenMP speedups are consistent with this. A defensible project contribution would be an honest scaling study (threads vs throughput alongside STREAM bandwidth, cache-resident vs DRAM-sized regions, matrix-first's single data pass reducing memory traffic relative to normal sequence) — matrix-first's bandwidth advantage under multithreading is plausible but I found no paper that measures it.
- Matrix-first reduces the number of passes over data (one instead of two), so in a bandwidth-bound multithreaded regime its advantage over the normal sequence should grow beyond what the operation-count model C = u + c·v predicts; this interaction (cost model ignoring memory traffic) is a potential genuinely new observation if the project measures it.

### Gaps
- No primary source found that plots CPU erasure-coding throughput vs thread count against measured memory bandwidth (i.e., explicit "more threads hurt" evidence); evidence is indirect (diminishing speedups, shrinking ISA-L advantage at 40 threads).
- Did not verify how Ceph's EC plugins thread encoding (per-PG/OSD-op parallelism rather than intra-stripe threading is my understanding [UNVERIFIED]).
- No pipelining-specific (CPU) erasure coding paper was retrieved this session; repair-pipelining work (e.g., ECPipe/repair pipelining, ATC 2017) concerns network repair, not intra-node compute [UNVERIFIED citation].
